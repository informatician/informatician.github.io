Opening, before students respond

“This is a quick class pulse. I want to understand the experiences and perspectives we already have in the room, and use them as a starting point for the course.”

  • Give students about 5–7 minutes.
  • Remind them that the optional open response should not identify an employer or disclose confidential information.
  • When time is up, open the dashboard and click Load / Refresh Results.

Overview tab

Start with the response count

“We have responses from most of the room, so this gives us a reasonable snapshot of the class.”

Use the three overview charts to move quickly from “who is here?” into the course themes. Avoid spending too long on every bar. Look for one or two patterns that are genuinely distinctive.

Academic Profile

Q1, Primary option/program

“BTM should dominate because this is the BTM capstone. The more interesting question is what other lenses are represented in the room.”

Q2, Additional option, microprogram, minor, or degree

“This shows the complementary perspectives people bring into the capstone, finance, analytics, consulting, sustainability, entrepreneurship, and others.”

Possible prompt: Does your second option or microprogram change how you tend to look at technology decisions?

Q4, Career interests

“Digital strategy matters across these career paths. It is relevant to consulting, finance, HR, public sector work, marketing, operations, and technology roles.”

Work Experience

Q5–Q6, Type and amount of experience

“This tells me how much practical workplace experience we can draw on during class discussions and cases.”

Q7, Sectors

Look for concentration in government, technology, financial services, consulting, or other sectors. Mention that examples from multiple sectors will improve discussion.

Q8, Functional exposure

“The class has seen technology from several functional viewpoints, not only from inside IT. That matters for a course focused on the integration of business and technology.”

Q9, Business–Technology Relationship

This is one of the strongest discussion questions in the survey because the choices form a rough progression from an IT-support relationship toward technology participating directly in business strategy.

“Notice where our workplace experiences cluster. Some of you have seen IT mainly as support, others have seen business and technology jointly shape priorities.”

Follow-up prompts:

  • What did that relationship look like in practice?
  • What made collaboration easier or harder?
  • Did the relationship differ depending on the decision being made?

“We will return to this directly when we discuss Business–IT alignment.”

Q10–Q11, Digital Transformation

Q10, “Digital transformation is primarily a technology challenge.”

“I am more interested in the spread of responses than in a single ‘correct’ answer. What assumptions sit behind the different positions?”

Ask one student from each side of the distribution to explain their reasoning, if the room is comfortable doing so.

Q11, Biggest obstacle

“This question forces prioritization. Several of these factors matter, but choosing one reveals what you believe tends to be the binding constraint.”

Useful probe: If you chose culture, leadership, legacy systems, or skills, what problem are you imagining?

Q12, First $1 Million of Digital Investment

“Treat this as a managerial allocation decision. The choice reflects where you think the organization’s biggest bottleneck is.”

Possible follow-ups:

  • Why would skills come before technology?
  • Why would data come before AI?
  • What would have to be true for cybersecurity to be the first investment?

Q13, Technology with Greatest Strategic Impact

“This is our beginning-of-semester forecast. We will revisit these assumptions when we discuss emerging technologies and digital innovation.”

If AI dominates, ask students what they mean by “impact”, productivity, business-model change, workforce change, customer experience, risk, or something else.

Q14, Digital Strategy Confidence

“This is a useful baseline. By the end of the capstone, I would expect you to be much more comfortable explaining digital strategy to a senior manager, and defending what you mean.”

Consider repeating this single question near the end of the semester as an informal before/after reflection.

Q15, Optional Open Responses

Scan the responses silently before reading any aloud. Even with instructions, students may inadvertently include identifiable details.

“These examples give us concrete situations to draw on. Does anyone recognize a broader pattern here without identifying the organization involved?”

Good themes to surface include workarounds, poor adoption, fragmented systems, data quality, automation benefits, digital friction, unclear governance, or unexpected uses of AI.

Closing the activity

“This profile gives us a starting point for the capstone. We have different academic lenses and different workplace experiences, and we already disagree on some important digital-strategy questions. We will use those differences throughout the course.”

Natural bridge to the course: cases, consulting simulations, and the field project all require students to integrate business and technology perspectives rather than treat them as separate domains.
